Hyperbaric oxygen — adjunct

Not on Dr. Sean’s sheet and not a substitute for anything on it. Your own addition: session 1 of 3 done Thu 20 Aug, 60 minutes — post-op day 1. Sessions 2 and 3 unscheduled. Own addition

The timing is the whole argument A transplanted follicular unit arrives with no blood supply. For roughly the first 48 hours it lives on plasmatic imbibition — fluid and dissolved oxygen diffusing in from the wound bed. Around days 2–4 its cut vessels link up with yours (inosculation), and new vessel growth finishes the job by about day 4–7. Oxygen delivery is diffusion-limited only during that window, and that limit is the one thing hyperbaric oxygen actually removes. Sessions on days 2 and 3 are worth far more than sessions on days 8 and 12 — so push the clinic to schedule the remaining two back-to-back this weekend rather than whenever is convenient.

Why pressure, not the mask, is what matters

Breathing room air your hemoglobin is already about 97% saturated, so a mask adds almost nothing to the oxygen your red cells carry. Under pressure the surplus dissolves directly into plasma instead (Henry’s law), and dissolved plasma oxygen is the only fraction that can diffuse into tissue with no capillaries of its own. Hemoglobin cannot deliver to a graft that has no vessels yet; plasma can. Pressure also stretches how far oxygen travels from the nearest perfused vessel — on the order of 60–70 µm normally, several times that under treatment — which is roughly the scale that separates a graft in a dense-packed recipient site from its nearest blood supply.

Find out the pressure before you rearrange your week A soft chamber at 1.3 ATA and a hard chamber at 2.0–2.4 ATA are not the same intervention — dissolved oxygen scales with pressure, so the gap between them is large, and the diffusion-radius benefit above largely belongs to the higher range. Ask what pressure and what oxygen fraction you were treated at. That single answer decides whether sessions 2 and 3 are worth moving other things for.

Honest limits

  • The mechanism is strong; the hair-transplant outcome data is thin. The solid evidence base is compromised flaps and grafts, diabetic ulcers, and radiation necrosis. The physiology transfers cleanly — a fresh graft is a compromised graft — but the trials were not run in FUE. Judgment
  • The ceiling is low because the baseline is high. Graft survival in competent hands is already around 90%+, so there is not much headroom. Any benefit lands on the marginal grafts, not the average one — which also means you will never be able to tell from your own result whether it helped. Judgment
  • It changes nothing else in the protocol. Do not let session scheduling displace the clinic’s washing schedule or the post-session follow-up. Nothing about hyperbaric oxygen interacts with Trintellix, olmesartan, rosuvastatin, or Keflex. Judgment
  • Practical risk at 3 × 60 min is ear and sinus barotrauma, not oxygen toxicity. Equalize on descent; say something if you are congested. Transient vision changes need far more sessions than three. Judgment

Exercise — return to training

The clinic sheet says only “no heavy exercise day before.” It gives no post-op activity rules, so everything below is reasoning from what is actually healing, not instruction — confirm the ramp at your follow-up. Confirm

Four separate things are being protected, and they resolve on different schedules, which is why a single “wait N weeks” answer is wrong. Bleeding at the recipient sites is the day 0–3 concern, since straining raises blood pressure before the sites have sealed. Sweat matters until the sites epithelialize over roughly 48–72 hours, after which salt and moisture stop being an infection and irritation risk. Mechanical contact is the longest-running one — anything that rubs, presses, or catches the grafts, including pulling a shirt over your head. And swelling peaks around day 2–4, so head-down positions and Valsalva straining make it visibly worse in that window.

One advantage of FUE here You have no linear donor closure under tension. With a strip procedure, neck flexion and upper-back work risk stretching or widening the scar for weeks. Extraction sites heal as small individual points instead, so range-of-motion is much less of a constraint for you than the general advice implies — your limits are blood pressure, sweat, and contact, not neck movement.
